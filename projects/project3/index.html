A · 1.1 Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

I sampled one noise tensor and reused it for t = 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 on my own photo (my cat, wearing a crown). The function returns exactly the noise at t = 0 and the image at t = 1.

At t = 0.25 only the brightest, largest structures survive (the yellow crown and the white door are faint blobs). By t = 0.5 the cat is recognizable, and t = 0.75 is mostly clean with grain on top.

A · 1.2 Classical Denoising

Baseline: undo the amplitude scaling with xt/t, then Gaussian blur. I swept σ ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} and kept the σ with the lowest MSE to the clean image.

A · 1.3 One-Step Denoising

One velocity prediction is enough to jump straight to an estimate of the clean image, x̂1 = xt + (1 − t) vθ(xt, t, c), with c = "a high quality photo".

A · 1.4 Visualizing Velocity

Here I compare what the model adds in one-step denoising, (1 − t) v̂, with the update that would recover my original exactly, (1 − t)(x1 − ε) = x1 − xt. The error map is the per-pixel RMS over RGB of their difference. Updates share one symmetric colour scale (0 = mid gray) and the error maps share one vmin/vmax across all three times.

A · 1.5 Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

Instead of one big jump, Euler integration takes many small steps xt+h = xt + h · v(xt, t), re-evaluating the velocity every time. The last step is shortened so the result lands exactly on t = 1. From t = 0.5 with h = 0.02 that's 25 model calls. I save the initial state, every 5th update and the final state. The notebook's checks pass (50 / 25 / 4 updates from t = 0 / 0.5 / 0.93, ending at 1).

Why Euler looks best but doesn't win on MSE

  • In the trajectory the noise fades steadily and the details sharpen late: the fur and the carpet only appear in the last couple of saved states.
  • One-step denoising outputs something close to the mean of all plausible clean images, and the mean is exactly what minimizes MSE. That's why it is soft but scores best.
  • Euler commits to one sharp, plausible sample. Its fur strands and door highlights are realistic, but they don't match my photo pixel-for-pixel, so MSE goes up slightly even though it looks much better.

A · 3.4 Hybrid Images

Factorized diffusion: take the low frequencies of prompt a's velocity and the high frequencies of prompt b's, at the same x and t. The low-pass is a Gaussian blur, kernel 265 and σ = 16 at 512×512. Far away (or small) you see prompt a; up close you see prompt b.

A · Bells & Whistles Visual Anagrams with Two More Transformations

Following Visual Anagrams (Geng et al.), the 3.3 trick works for any view A that is an orthogonal linear map of the pixels: you average each view's velocity after mapping it back with A−1. I added two views from the paper:

  • 90° rotation (clockwise): a global pixel permutation.
  • Inner-circle rotation: only a central disk (60% of the width) rotates by 90°, and the outer ring stays put. The disk is measured from the exact grid centre (255.5, 255.5), so it maps onto itself under a 90° rotation and the view is an exact permutation.

1.2.1 Training

Trained to map z (σ = 0.5) back to x with an L2 loss. Batch 256, 5 epochs, D = 128, Adam lr 1e-4, shuffled training set, and fresh noise drawn every time a batch is fetched. Mean loss per epoch: 0.0313 → 0.0161 → 0.0111 → 0.0093 → 0.0121. The last epoch bumps up a bit, which is ordinary minibatch noise at this lr; the samples still improve.

After epoch 1 the digits are already readable but blotchy, with background speckle. After epoch 5 strokes are clean and the background is black. Some ambiguous inputs (the "5" in column 9) come out as a smudge, since the noise destroyed too much to tell.

B · 1.2.3 Denoising Pure Noise

Same setup, but the input is pure noise z = ε and the target is still a clean digit. The loss flat-lines at about 0.0675 for all 5 epochs.

What the pattern is, and why

  • Every output is the same blurry ghost digit, a fuzzy oval with hints of 0, 3, 8 and 9 overlaid, no matter which noise goes in. It matches the mean of the training set: the largest pixel difference is 0.037 and the outputs vary by only 0.004 (std) across inputs.
  • Under an MSE loss the best possible prediction is the conditional expectation E[x | z]. Here z is pure noise, independent of x, so E[x | z] = E[x]: the average digit. The network learns to ignore its input and output that constant. That's also why the loss can't go below ≈ 0.0675, which is the pixel variance of MNIST around its mean.

B · 2.1–2.3 Time-Conditioned Flow Matching

Training: batch 64, D = 64, Adam lr 1e-2 with ExponentialLR γ = 0.11/10 (stepped once per epoch, 1e-2 → 1e-3), 10 epochs, with noise and t drawn fresh for every batch. Mean loss per epoch fell from 0.166 to 0.091.

Samples, 50 Euler steps, same seed each time

After epoch 1 the samples are thin, broken strokes. By epoch 10 most are legible digits (9, 1, 3, 0, 6, 8, 5…), but some are still malformed (a "7." with a stray dot, a squiggle in row 2). The model only knows the time, not which digit it's drawing, and it has no way to commit early.

B · 2.4–2.6 Class-Conditioned Flow Matching

Two more FCBlocks take a one-hot class vector c, and the conditioning becomes unflatten = c1·unflatten + t1 and up1 = c2·up1 + t2. With probability 0.1 the class vector is zeroed during training, so the same network also learns the unconditional flow. That is what enables classifier-free guidance at sampling time.

Same optimizer and schedule as 2.2 (batch 64, D = 64, Adam 1e-2 → 1e-3, 10 epochs). Loss fell from 0.159 to 0.085.

4 samples of each digit, γ = 5, 300 steps, fixed seed

Conditioning plus guidance helps a lot. Even after one epoch every column is the right digit. A few are rough (the 8s and 9s, a broken 5), and by epoch 10 they're all clean, thick, well-formed and still varied in slant and style within each column.

B · 2.6 Getting Rid of the Learning-Rate Scheduler

The exponential schedule starts at 1e-2 (fast progress) and ends at 1e-3 (small, stable updates). Without it, a constant 1e-2 would keep taking big, noisy steps all the way to the end. To compensate I used a constant lr of 1e-3 (the value the schedule decays to) and added gradient-norm clipping at 1.0 to guard against occasional spikes. Everything else is identical.

Result

  • Final-epoch mean loss is 0.0844 without the scheduler vs. 0.0854 with it, and the epoch-10 samples look just as good: clean digits in every column.
  • The constant-lr run is even ahead early on. Adam already scales each parameter's step, so 1e-3 is plenty for this small model, and 1e-2 mostly buys speed in the first few hundred steps at the cost of noise. Clipping keeps the rare large-gradient batches from undoing progress.

To measure "better" instead of eyeballing it, I trained a small CNN classifier on MNIST (98.6% test accuracy) and ran it on 1000 samples from each model. A good sample is one the classifier is confident about; class entropy checks that all ten digits still appear (the maximum is ln 10 = 2.303).

Takeaways

  • The share of ambiguous samples (confidence ≤ 0.9) drops from 22% to 13%, and diversity goes slightly up, so it isn't cheating by drawing only easy digits. Visually, the baseline's squiggles and stray marks are mostly gone: same noise, same layout, cleaner strokes.
  • FiLM matters most. Time can now shift features as well as scale them, at every resolution. EMA smooths out the last bit of optimizer noise.
  • More Euler steps (300 vs. 50) barely help. The remaining errors come from the model, not from integration error.
