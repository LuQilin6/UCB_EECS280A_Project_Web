Project 1 — CS280A

Images of the Russian Empire: Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Collection

Splitting, aligning, and recombining three grayscale glass-plate exposures (B, G, R) into single color photographs — automatically.

Part 1 Single-Scale Alignment

For the small .jpg scans, I exhaustively search every integer displacement in a [-15, 15] window on both axes, scoring each candidate shift of the green and red channels against the blue channel with two metrics: L2 / Euclidean distance (sqrt(sum((image1 - image2)^2)), lower is better) and Normalized Cross-Correlation (a dot product of mean-subtracted, unit-normalized channels, higher is better). The best-scoring (dx, dy) shift is applied with np.roll before stacking the three channels into one RGB image.

Part 2 Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

Exhaustive search becomes far too slow on the full-resolution .tif scans, where the true displacement can be hundreds of pixels. Instead I build an image pyramid: each image is repeatedly downsampled by 2× (via sk.transform.rescale) until it's small enough to align exhaustively, then the estimated shift is doubled and refined with a small [-2, 2] window while moving back down to full resolution. All 14 example images below were aligned this way, on RGB pixel values, with both the L2 and NCC metrics — shown here after the full cropping / contrast / white-balance / color-mapping pipeline (Part 4).

Part 3 Where It Broke: emir.tif

🖊️ Why did alignment fail here?

Out of all 14 example images, only emir.tif (the Emir of Bukhara) fails to align under the pyramid pipeline — its red-channel shift comes out as roughly (-606, -278) or (33, -268) depending on the metric, wildly outside the range every other image falls in (all within a few dozen to ~180 pixels). emir.tif was the hardest image in the set to align. Unlike a simple brightness offset between channels, the Emir's clothing and turban genuinely look different across R, G, and B( we can also notice this from the original image). It's a region can appear bright in one channel and dark in another purely because of its color. Raw-pixel similarity metrics (NCC/L2) have no reliable signal to latch onto there. Switching to gradient/edge-based alignment substantially fixed this, since object boundaries tend to occur in the same place across all three channels regardless of color(shown in part 4.5).

🖊️ Approach

If we only combine the three layers directly, we will notice that there are borders and make the picture looks bad. In this way, we want to crop the image. Rather than slicing off a fixed number of border pixels, I detect the border by measuring per-pixel disagreement between the R, G, and B channels (max(R,G,B) − min(R,G,B)), based on the insight that well-aligned interior content shows fairly consistent color across channels while the border shows strong channel disagreement. I compute a "normal" baseline from the image's interior, then scan inward from each of the four edges (up to a bounded fraction of that dimension) and crop through the last pixel whose disagreement still exceeds 1.5× that baseline, rather than the first dip below it. This is because the border can contain multiple bands with transient dips in between. We can see from the before/after pairs that this approach successfully crops out the border while preserving the interior content.

🖊️ Approach

We don't want the pixel value in a picture be too large or too small, in this way, we need to implement contrast enhancement. I apply a single global linear rescale, (im − im.min()) / (im.max() − im.min()), using the minimum and maximum taken across all three channels together rather than per channel. Using a shared global min/max (instead of stretching each channel independently) is what keeps this purely a contrast operation rather than accidentally also performing white balance. It expands the image to fill the full [0, 1] range while preserving the original relative balance between channels.

🖊️ Approach

We also want the image's white/gray to appear neutral. This is why we want to do white balance. I estimate the scene illuminant two different ways and correct for it with a per-channel gain. The gray_world method assumes the image's average color should be neutral, and computes each channel's gain as overall_mean / channel_mean。 It equalizd the channels while preserving overall brightness. The white_patch method instead assumes the image's brightest pixel represents true white, and scales each channel so its maximum value hits exactly 1. In practice gray_world was the more useful of the two for correcting the mild yellow/warm cast visible in our aligned outputs. White-patch is more easily thrown off by a single bright highlight in these scans.

🖊️ Approach

Instead of assuming the plate's physical R/G/B filters correspond directly to the RGB primaries, I applied a full 3×3 linear mixing matrix to the pixel values. I implemented this by using a manually-specified matrix with a diagonal per-channel boost [1.1, 1.2, 1.2]. This is after several rounds of trial-and-error tuning to make the final images look more like the original glass-plate exposures as the original pictures tend to be yellowish and dark. What the matrix does is to boost the green and blue channels more than the red channel, which is consistent with the original color cast of the glass plates. From the comparison of images, we can see that the color mapping step makes the images look more natural and bright.

🖊️ Approach

For images where raw pixel intensities disagree structurally across channels. In this way, I align on gradient-magnitude edge maps instead of raw pixels. I computed horizontal and vertical intensity gradients, using a small 3×3 weighted-difference kernel applied via padding and shifted-array slicing, and combined them into a single edge-strength map as sqrt(Gx² + Gy²). We then fed these edge maps into our existing NCC/L2 search machinery unchanged. This worked because object boundaries tend to occur in the same place across R, G, and B regardless of each region's color, making edges far more reliable than raw intensity for exactly the cases where color-based matching breaks down. We can see this from emir.tif, where the alignment is much better after switching to gradient features.
